Irregular Periods: How Acupuncture and Moxibustion Are Used in Traditional Chinese Medicine

Back to the blog

25 November 2026 · Liping Jiang

If your menstrual cycle has become irregular, it is understandable to want a clear explanation of what that can mean, and of the options available to you. This article is a general introduction to how Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) theory talks about the menstrual cycle and irregularity, and what acupuncture and moxibustion involve if you visit a clinic. These are traditional concepts, not medical facts. This is not a claim that acupuncture or moxibustion treats, cures, regulates or fixes irregular periods, and it is not a substitute for medical assessment.

See your doctor first

An irregular cycle can have many medical causes. These can include pregnancy, thyroid conditions, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), changes around puberty or perimenopause, stress, significant weight change, certain medicines, and other gynaecological or hormonal conditions. Only a doctor can investigate these properly.

If your periods have become irregular, very heavy, very painful or have stopped, or if you are worried you could be pregnant, please see your general practitioner. Your GP may arrange tests or refer you to a gynaecologist. Seek urgent medical care, or call 000, if you have severe pain, very heavy bleeding, fainting, or other symptoms that feel urgent.

If you later choose to try acupuncture or moxibustion, they can be used alongside the care your doctor provides. They are not a replacement for medical investigation or treatment, and I will not suggest that you stop or change any care your doctor has arranged.

How Traditional Chinese Medicine theory views the menstrual cycle

In Traditional Chinese Medicine, the menstrual cycle is often described in terms of qi (sometimes explained as the body’s functional activity), blood, and warmth, and the way these are thought to move through the body. This is a traditional way of talking about health. It is not the same as a medical description of hormones, the ovaries or the uterus.

TCM theory often relates the cycle to several organ systems as they are understood in that tradition, particularly the Kidney, Liver and Spleen:

  • The Kidney is associated with essence and the longer foundation of reproductive health, as those ideas are used in Chinese medicine.
  • The Liver is associated with the smooth movement of qi and blood.
  • The Spleen is associated with the production and holding of blood.

In this traditional view, a regular cycle is thought to depend on qi and blood being ample and moving smoothly, with enough warmth to support that movement. These are TCM concepts used to guide a traditional assessment. They are not a medical diagnosis.

How TCM theory describes irregularity

When a cycle is early, late, unpredictable, or very light or heavy, a TCM practitioner may discuss it in terms of traditional patterns. Examples you might hear include qi stagnation, blood stasis, a deficiency of qi or blood, Kidney deficiency, or an accumulation of cold or dampness, as those ideas are used in Chinese medicine.

This language helps a practitioner organise a traditional assessment. It does not identify a medical cause, and it does not replace tests or a diagnosis from your GP or gynaecologist. Two people with similar cycle changes may be described quite differently in TCM, because the practitioner also looks at sleep, digestion, energy, temperature preference, stress and other details.

What acupuncture involves

Acupuncture uses fine, sterile, single-use needles at selected points on the body. In a visit that discusses menstrual health, points may be chosen on the abdomen, legs, arms or back, depending on the traditional assessment and on what is comfortable for you.

The needles are usually left in place for a time while you rest, often around 25 to 35 minutes. Many people feel a dull ache, warmth or tingling at a point; some feel very little. You can ask for a needle to be adjusted or removed at any time. I will explain what I am doing as we go.

What moxibustion involves

Moxibustion is a traditional method that uses gentle heat, usually from processed mugwort (moxa), near selected points. In TCM theory, this warmth is thought to move qi and blood and to disperse cold, as those ideas are understood in the tradition. That is a traditional explanation, not a statement of medical effect.

At Lotus Acupuncture I use a smokeless moxa machine, so there is no smoke from burning moxa in the treatment room. Moxibustion may be offered as a session on its own, or together with acupuncture, if it seems appropriate and you are comfortable with the warmth.

Please tell me if you are pregnant, or think you might be, or if you are sensitive to heat. Moxibustion is not used in every situation.
